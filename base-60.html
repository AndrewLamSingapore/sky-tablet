The Mathematics of the Heavens: Mesopotamia's Base-60 Legacy

Interpretation Lens · Historical knowledge · Mathematics and astronomy

Before mechanical clocks and modern observatories, Mesopotamian scribes developed a mathematical tradition that could divide a whole into sixty parts. Its enduring legacy is familiar: sixty seconds in a minute, sixty minutes in an hour, and 360 degrees in a circle.

What does base-60 mean?

Our everyday decimal system groups quantities by tens. A sexagesimal, or base-60, positional system groups them by sixties. In positional notation, each place is worth sixty times the place to its right. Thus 1,20 in sexagesimal notation means 1 × 60 + 20 = 80 in decimal notation, not 1.20. Similarly, 2,30 means 150.

A sexagesimal fraction such as 0;30 means 30/60, or one-half. Modern scholars often use a semicolon to distinguish whole-number places from fractional places; this is a modern transcription convention, not an ancient punctuation mark.

The system was especially convenient because 60 has many divisors: 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. Halves, thirds, quarters, fifths and sixths can all be represented cleanly.

From records to mathematical knowledge

Mesopotamian mathematics emerged across a long history involving Sumerian and later Babylonian scribal cultures. Early administrative records tracked goods, labor, land and obligations. Numerical practices were not uniform across all periods and purposes: multiple metrological and counting systems existed before the fully developed sexagesimal place-value tradition associated especially with Old Babylonian mathematics.

On clay tablets, trained scribes carried out multiplication, division and problems involving areas, volumes and reciprocal tables. Sexagesimal place value made sophisticated calculations possible without our modern decimal notation. Early writing did not always provide an unambiguous zero placeholder or a fixed sign distinguishing integer and fractional positions; context mattered.

Measuring the sky

The sky mattered for calendars, ritual, agriculture and the ordering of civic life. Over centuries, Mesopotamian astronomers recorded celestial phenomena and developed mathematical methods for describing and predicting recurrent patterns. Later Babylonian astronomical computation made extensive use of sexagesimal fractions.

The astronomical compendium MUL.APIN preserves organized star and calendar knowledge. It should not, however, be treated as proof that every visible constellation, planet position or architectural feature in this 3D world represents a precisely dated observation.

The connection is deeper than a convenient counting trick: observation became record, record became calculation, and calculation became a means of anticipating cycles.

Why we still count in sixties

The modern hour's sixty minutes and the minute's sixty seconds belong to a long history of astronomical and mathematical conventions transmitted and adapted across civilizations. Angular measurement also retains a 360-degree circle, with each degree divided into sixty arcminutes and each arcminute into sixty arcseconds.

It would be too simple to claim that every modern convention was invented at once by the Sumerians. These practices evolved through Babylonian astronomy and later Greek, Islamic and other mathematical traditions. Their survival demonstrates how useful systems can outlast the societies that first developed them.

Read a sexagesimal number

Try it: If one hour has sixty minutes, what is 1;30 hours? It is one hour plus 30/60 of an hour: 90 minutes. Here the semicolon marks the fractional part of the hour.
