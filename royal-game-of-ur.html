The Royal Game of Ur

A Mesopotamian board game known from archaeological boards, including the celebrated example from the Royal Cemetery at Ur. Surviving evidence establishes the game’s antiquity; the precise ancient rules remain a subject of reconstruction.

Play a reconstruction

This is a modern Finkel-style teaching reconstruction, not a claim to reproduce the exact rules used at Ur. Two players race seven pieces across a shared track. The computer makes legal moves automatically. This version uses seven pieces per player, four binary dice, a protected central rosette, captures on other shared squares, and extra turns for rosettes.

Illustrated board guide

Board orientation: Ivory uses the top row; Indigo uses the bottom row. Both move through the eight-square shared middle row in the same direction. The five gold ✺ squares are rosettes.

Follow the numbered journey: ① enter at the private row near the gap (column 5), ② move left along four private squares, ③ travel right through the entire middle row, ④ turn back left across the two private finishing squares, ⑤ bear off with an exact roll. Both players follow mirrored private routes.

Dice probability: 0 = 1/16 · 1 = 4/16 · 2 = 6/16 · 3 = 4/16 · 4 = 1/16. These are modern mathematical probabilities for four fair binary dice, not evidence of an ancient probability theory.

How to move: Roll, then tap a green-outlined destination square. The game selects the eligible piece automatically. A green square is a legal destination, not a suggested move.

How this teaching variant works

  1. Each player starts with seven pieces off the board. First to finish all seven wins.
  2. Four two-sided dice yield a roll from 0 to 4; each die contributes one point when marked.
  3. Choose a piece to enter or advance exactly the rolled number of spaces. A roll of zero loses the turn.
  4. Land on an opponent’s piece in the shared middle row to send it back to the start, except on the protected central rosette.
  5. Landing on a rosette earns another turn. An exact roll is required to leave the track.

Read the evidence through three lenses

Evidence-based

Physical game boards and pieces attest to ancient board gaming in Mesopotamia. A much later Babylonian cuneiform rules text studied by Irving Finkel informs reconstruction of the wider Game of Twenty Squares tradition; it does not document every rule of the earlier Ur board.

Interpretive

The 14-step path, seven pieces per side, four binary dice, captures, rosette bonus turns, protected central rosette and exact-roll exit follow a widely played modern Finkel-style reconstruction. These rules should not be confused with a complete surviving third-millennium rulebook. Other published reconstructions differ.

Imagined extension

The computer opponent, visual interface, and any future immersive chamber are modern creations. A virtual tomb or temple scene should not be presented as the historically verified location where this game was played.

What the VR research contributes

The paper The Royal Game of Ur: Virtual Reality Prototype of the Board Game Played in Ancient Mesopotamia, by Krzysztof Pietroszek, Zaki Agraraharja, and Christian Eckhardt, describes an immersive prototype with a Unity environment, AI opponent, and interaction experiments including hand tracking. It is a useful precedent for experience design, not a primary source for the ancient rules and not a license to reuse its assets.
